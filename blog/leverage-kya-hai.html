Leverage

What Is Leverage in Trading? A Beginner's Guide

LotCal Blog6 min read

What Is Leverage, in Plain Words?

Leverage is a feature your broker gives you that lets you control a trading position worth far more than the money sitting in your account. Instead of needing the full price of a contract, you only need to put up a fraction of it, and the broker covers the rest. That small slice you put up is called your margin.

Think of it like this. If you want to buy a car that costs $10,000 but you only have $1,000, you cannot buy it outright. But if a friend agrees to lend you the other $9,000, you can drive the car home while having only committed your own $1,000. You are using leverage. Trading leverage works on the same principle, except the "friend" is your broker and the "car" is a futures contract or another traded asset.

The important part is that you are not borrowing the money to spend it. You are using it to control a position. The profit and loss, though, are calculated on the full size of the position, not just on the money you put in.

What Do the Numbers Like 1:10 and 1:100 Mean?

The ratio tells you how much buying power you get for every dollar of your own money. A leverage of 1:10 means that for every $1 you commit, you control $10 worth of position. A leverage of 1:100 means every $1 controls $100. A ratio of 1:20, 1:50, or 1:200 all work the same way — the second number is simply how many times your own money is multiplied.

Different brokers and different products offer different maximum ratios. Futures contracts often come with built-in leverage because the exchange sets the margin requirement, while some brokers let you choose your own leverage level up to a limit. You will often see these written as "10x," "50x," or "100x."

A Worked Example with Real Numbers

Let us say you have $100 in your account and your broker offers leverage of 1:100. That means your $100 can control a position worth $10,000. The math is simple:

Position value = account balance × leverage

$100 × 100 = $10,000

Now imagine the asset moves up by 1%. On a $10,000 position, that is a profit of $100. Your account balance has doubled from $100 to $200, even though the asset itself barely moved. That is the appeal of leverage — small price moves become large percentage swings in your account.

But the same works in reverse. If the asset moves down by 1%, you lose $100, and your $100 balance is gone. So a 1% move at 1:100 leverage is enough to wipe out your entire margin. Keep that in mind — we come back to it in the risk article.

The Simple Formula Behind It

The way to think about your effective leverage at any moment is:

Leverage used = position value ÷ account balance

If you control $10,000 with a $500 balance, your leverage is 20x. If you control $10,000 with a $200 balance, your leverage is 50x. Notice that the leverage you are actually using depends on both the size of the position and the balance you have left. As your balance grows or shrinks, the same position becomes more or less leveraged for you.

This is a useful habit to build: check your effective leverage before you open a trade, not just the maximum your broker advertises.

Why Do Brokers Offer Leverage at All?

It can seem strange that a broker would let you control so much more money than you have. There are a few reasons.

First, it makes markets accessible. A single futures contract can be worth a lot of money. Without leverage, most retail traders could never participate. Leverage lets a smaller trader take part with a realistic account size.

Second, the broker makes money from your trading — through spreads, commissions, or fees — and leverage encourages more trading activity. More position size, more activity, more business for the broker.

Third, and most important to understand, the broker is not really giving you free money. The broker is lending you the difference, and it protects itself by holding your margin as collateral. If the position moves against you far enough, the broker closes it automatically to protect its own funds. This is why leverage is always tied to a margin requirement.

The Catch: Two Sides of the Same Coin

Leverage multiplies both profits and losses. It does not make you a better trader. It simply makes the size of your swings bigger. A strategy that would lose a little without leverage can lose everything with it, and a strategy that would win a little can win a lot.

This is the part that trips up beginners. The temptation is to use maximum leverage because it feels like free power. But the real question is not "how much can I make?" It is "how much of a price move can I survive?" At 1:100, a 1% move against you ends the trade. At 1:20, you can absorb a 5% move before your margin is gone.

Many experienced traders deliberately use far less than the maximum allowed. They size their positions so that even a normal swing in the market does not threaten their whole account.

A Simple Takeaway

Leverage is a magnifying glass, not a money printer. It gives you access and buying power, and it multiplies whatever outcome your trading would have produced anyway. If you are new to this, start with a low ratio, keep position sizes small, and always know how much room you have before your margin is at risk.
