Gold Contract Size: What 1 Lot of XAUUSD Really Is

LotCal Blog6 min read

What "1 lot" actually controls

When you open a gold trade and your broker says you bought 1 lot of XAUUSD, you are not buying "one gold." You are buying a contract that controls a fixed amount of physical gold — and for the vast majority of retail brokers that fixed amount is 100 troy ounces.

A troy ounce is not the same as the ounce you weigh food in. The troy system is the one precious metals have used for centuries, and it is slightly heavier: one troy ounce is about 31.1 grams, versus 28.3 grams for a standard ounce. It matters only because the market quotes gold in troy ounces, so every number you see on screen is already in troy units.

So the whole game comes down to this: 1 lot = 100 oz. Everything else — your position value, your profit, your loss — flows from that single fact. Once you hold onto it, the rest of gold sizing is just multiplication.

The two numbers that matter

There are really only two calculations you need to make sense of a gold position, and both start with the same idea: multiply the ounces you control by the price.

Position value

Your position value is simply the ounces you control multiplied by the current gold price.

  • 1.00 lot = 100 oz. At $4,100 per oz, your position is worth 100 × 4100 = $410,000.
  • 0.10 lot = 10 oz. At the same price: 10 × 4100 = $41,000.
  • 0.01 lot = 1 oz. That is 1 × 4100 = $4,100.

This is the number that tells you how big your exposure really is. A 0.01 lot gold trade at $4,100 is a $4,100 position even if your account balance is far smaller. That gap between what you control and what you own is exactly what leverage is doing — and it is why the position value matters more than the lot number.

Profit and loss per $1 move

Because each lot controls 100 ounces, a one-dollar move in the gold price moves your P&L by $100 per lot. The formula is clean:

P&L per $1 move = lot size × 100

  • 1.00 lot → $100 per $1 of gold movement.
  • 0.10 lot → $10 per $1.
  • 0.01 lot → $1 per $1.
  • 0.001 lot → $0.10 per $1.

That last row is the most useful one for small accounts. If gold moves $10 in your favour while you hold 0.01 lot, you made $10. If it moves $10 against you, you lost $10. The relationship is perfectly linear, which is the whole reason gold is so easy to reason about once you know the contract size.

A worked example with a small account

Let's make it concrete. Say your account has $30 in it and you decide to buy 0.01 lot of gold at $4,100.

Position value: 1 oz × 4100 = $4,100. You are controlling $4,100 of gold with $30 of your own money. That is roughly 137× leverage — far beyond what most brokers would allow on a single position, and a genuinely enormous amount of risk to take on.

Now watch what happens on the P&L side. Gold moves $1 up: you are up $1. Gold moves $10 up: you are up $10. Gold moves $50 against you: you are down $50 — but you only have $30 in the account, so that trade would have closed you out long before the price ever got there.

This is not a lecture about whether you should trade gold with a tiny balance. It is the arithmetic, and the arithmetic says a $30 account cannot really absorb even a $10 adverse move on 0.01 lot without running straight into its margin. The contract size did not change — you always knew 0.01 lot was 1 ounce — but the leverage math is where that fact becomes impossible to ignore.

Fractional lots and the "1 ounce" shortcut

Here is a handy mental shortcut: on gold, 0.01 lot = 1 ounce. That makes the arithmetic easy. Every 0.01 lot you add is another ounce, and each ounce is worth roughly $4,100 at current prices. So a 0.05 lot trade is 5 ounces — a position value of about $20,500.

Many brokers let you go below 0.01 lot, and some will take you all the way down to 0.001 or smaller. The same rule applies: 0.001 lot is 0.1 ounce, and its P&L per $1 move is exactly $0.10.

For any fraction, the pattern holds: multiply the lot number by 100 to get the ounces, then multiply the ounces by the price for the position value, and multiply the lot number by 100 again for the per-dollar P&L.

Contract sizes vary — always check

Everything above assumes the standard: 100 troy ounces per lot. That is the most common arrangement, but it is not universal. Some brokers quote gold in different units, some use a different base pair, and some futures exchanges define their gold contract differently from spot trading. The CME's gold futures contract, for example, is also 100 troy ounces, but the way margin and settlement work is not the same as a spot CFD.

Bottom line

Gold contract size is one of those facts that looks intimidating and is actually simple. One lot controls 100 ounces. Position value is ounces times price. P&L moves $100 per $1 of price movement on a full lot, and it scales down cleanly from there. Once you have internalised the "0.01 lot = 1 oz = $1 per $1 move" trio, you can size a gold position in your head — and you will be far less likely to accidentally control $410,000 with an account that was never built to handle it.
