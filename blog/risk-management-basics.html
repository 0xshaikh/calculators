Risk Management Basics: The 1–2% Rule and Stop Losses

LotCal Blog7 min read

Why risk management beats prediction

Every trader has a story about the trade that was "obviously" going to work. The chart setup was clean, the news lined up, the indicators all agreed. Then the market went the other way, and the account took a hit. The uncomfortable truth is that nobody predicts markets reliably — not even professionals. What separates traders who survive from traders who blow up is not prediction. It is risk management.

Risk management is a set of decisions you make before the trade: how much money you are willing to lose if the trade fails, where you will get out if you are wrong, and how much you expect to make if you are right. You can be wrong often and still grow an account, as long as each wrong trade costs little and each right trade earns more. That single idea carries the rest of this article.

The 1–2% rule

The 1–2% rule says the amount you risk on any one trade should be no more than 1% to 2% of your trading balance. "Risk" here means the money you lose if your stop loss is hit — not the size of your position, and not the margin you put up. If you have a $10,000 account and you risk 1%, a stop-out costs you $100. If you risk 2%, it costs you $200.

The number sounds small, and that is the point. Risking 1% means a normal run of losing trades is survivable. Risking 10% or 20% means a handful of losses can gut your account — and give you no real chance to recover. The 1–2% rule exists because the market does not care how confident you feel.

Where stop losses go

A stop loss is not a random number. It belongs where your trade idea stops being true. If you bought because price bounced off a support level, the stop goes just below that support. If you sold because price hit a resistance area, the stop goes just above it. The stop marks the point where your reason for being in the trade no longer holds, so if it is hit, you get out and move on.

Placing stops at round numbers or arbitrary dollar amounts is a common mistake. Price often sweeps a level before reversing, and a stop set too close gets taken out by ordinary noise. But a stop set too far away makes the trade too expensive in risk terms. This is where position size earns its keep: you adjust the size of your position so the distance from entry to stop equals your fixed 1% or 2% risk, whatever that distance turns out to be.

Risk/reward at 1:2

A risk/reward ratio compares what you risk to what you expect to gain. At 1:2, you risk $100 to try to make $200. At 1:3, you risk $100 to try to make $300. The higher the ratio, the more of your losers your winners have to cover.

Here is why 1:2 matters. If your average win is twice your average loss, you can be wrong more than half the time and still make money. You do not need a high win rate. You need winners that are meaningfully bigger than losers, and the discipline to take the loss when the stop is hit.

A worked example: 1% risk on a $10,000 account

Suppose your account is $10,000 and you risk 1%, so your risk budget is $100. You are trading a micro contract where each point of price movement is worth $1 per contract.

You spot a setup: price has bounced off a support level at 4,200. You plan to enter at 4,220 and place your stop at 4,190, just below support. The distance from entry to stop is 30 points.

With a $1 point value, one micro contract loses $30 if the stop is hit. Your $100 budget divided by $30 is about 3.3, so you take 3 micro contracts. Your actual risk is 3 × $30 = $90, comfortably under your 1% limit. If the distance were wider, you would take fewer contracts; if narrower, more. That is position sizing in action.

Now set a target at 1:2. A 2:1 reward means a target 60 points away, at 4,280. If price reaches it, you make 3 × $60 = $180. You are risking $90 to make $180 — a 1:2 trade, sized to your account.

Why a 50% win rate with 1:2 reward is profitable

Over 100 trades at a 50% win rate with 1:2 reward: 50 winners at +2R and 50 losers at −1R. The math is 50 × 2 minus 50 × 1, which equals +100R. With R equal to the $90 risk in our example, that is about +$9,000 on a $10,000 account before costs.

The break-even win rate for a 1:2 ratio is one trade in three, or about 33%. Win more than a third of your trades at 1:2 and you are profitable, even if you lose more often than you win. That is why the ratio matters more than the streak of green or red on your screen.

Loss-streak math: 2% risk versus 10% risk

This is the part worth memorising. Two traders each start with $10,000. Trader A risks 2% per trade. Trader B risks 10% per trade. Both hit a losing streak of five trades in a row.

Trader A compounds down 2% each time: 10,000 × 0.98 × 0.98 × 0.98 × 0.98 × 0.98 ≈ $9,039. That is a drop of about $961, or roughly 9.6%. To get back to $10,000, Trader A needs a gain of about 10.6% on the remaining balance.

Trader B compounds down 10% each time: 10,000 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 ≈ $5,905. That is a drop of about $4,095, or roughly 41%. To get back to $10,000, Trader B needs a gain of about 69% on what is left.

The same five losing trades leave one trader needing a modest bounce and the other needing a near-doubling just to break even. Risk per trade decides which one you are. A 2% risk account can absorb a bad streak; a 10% risk account is one streak away from deep trouble. This is why the 1–2% rule, a real stop at structure, and a clear 1:2 target — not a crystal ball — are what keep a futures trader alive.
