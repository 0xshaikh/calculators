Forex Contract Sizes: EURUSD, GBPUSD, and the 100,000-Unit Standard

LotCal Blog6 min read

One lot is a fixed bundle of currency

Every market has a standard unit of measurement. In shares it is the share. In futures it is the contract. In forex, the standard unit is called a lot, and one standard lot is defined as 100,000 units of the base currency. That is the first thing to get straight, because almost everything else in position sizing depends on it.

The base currency is the first currency in a pair. In EURUSD, the base is the euro. In GBPUSD, the base is the British pound. So when you buy one standard lot of EURUSD, you are not buying 100,000 dollars — you are buying 100,000 euros, and you are paying for them with dollars. The pair's price simply tells you how many dollars those 100,000 euros are worth at that moment.

Position value is units times price

For any pair where the base currency is the first one, position value follows one simple formula: multiply the number of units by the current price. If EURUSD is trading at 1.0850 and you hold one standard lot, you control 100,000 euros, and the dollar value of that position is 100,000 times 1.0850, which comes to $108,500.

That number matters because it is what your margin and your risk are calculated against. A trade on one lot of EURUSD at 1.0850 is not a small trade. It controls about $108,500 worth of currency, even though you might only be required to put down a few thousand dollars of margin. The contract size is what turns a tiny price move into a meaningful amount of money.

What a 0.01 lot actually is

Brokers let you trade fractions of a lot, and the smallest standard fraction is 0.01 lots, sometimes called a micro lot. That is 1,000 units of the base currency. So 0.10 lots is 10,000 units, 0.50 lots is 50,000 units, and 1.00 lots is the full 100,000 units.

This is why the lot size on your order ticket is usually written with two decimal places. It is not a separate unit — it is just a decimal fraction of one standard lot. When you see 0.25 lots on a ticket, you are looking at 25,000 units. When you see 1.50, you are looking at 150,000 units.

The majors: where the base currency comes first

Most of the pairs you will trade belong to a group called the majors, and most of them are quoted with the base currency first. EURUSD, GBPUSD, AUDUSD, and NZDUSD all work this way. For all of them, position value is units multiplied by the price, because the first currency in the pair is the one you are buying or selling a fixed quantity of.

There is a useful shortcut here. For these pairs, the position value in dollars is always just the lot size times 100,000 times the current price. It sounds like a lot of arithmetic, but it collapses into something you can do in your head. One lot of GBPUSD at 1.2700 is 127,000 dollars. Half a lot is 63,500 dollars. A quarter lot is 31,750 dollars.

USD-based pairs flip the calculation

Now the pairs that start with the dollar: USDJPY, USDCHF, and USDCAD. Here the base currency is the US dollar, so one standard lot is 100,000 dollars. The second currency — the yen, the franc, the loonie — is the one you are converting into, and the price tells you how much of it one dollar buys.

This changes the position value math in a subtle but important way. For these pairs, you already know the value of the base side: it is always 100,000 dollars for a full lot, no matter what the price is doing. The price does not change how many dollars you are controlling. It only changes how many yen, francs, or Canadian dollars you would receive in exchange.

So the shortcut for USD-based pairs is different: position value is lot size times 100,000, and the price is not part of that figure. This trips up a lot of beginners who try to apply the EURUSD formula to USDJPY and end up with a position value that is wildly wrong. For USDJPY at 150.00, one lot is still just 100,000 dollars of value — not 100,000 times 150.

Why pip value depends on the pair

The contract size also drives your pip value, which is how much money a one-pip move is worth. For EURUSD, a pip is the fourth decimal place, and on one standard lot it is worth roughly 10 dollars. The same is true for GBPUSD, AUDUSD, and NZDUSD at their standard sizes.

For USDJPY, a pip is the second decimal place, and the value on a standard lot is not a fixed 10 dollars. It moves with the price, because the pair is quoted in yen and the pip value has to be converted back into dollars. This is exactly why so many traders use a calculator rather than trying to hold all these conversions in their head. The contract size is the anchor, but the quote currency and the current rate both feed into the final number.

The practical takeaway

The single most useful thing to remember is that one standard lot always means 100,000 units of the base currency. From that one fact, everything else follows. If you can hold onto that, you can work out position value, pip value, and risk for any pair, whether it is EURUSD or USDJPY, in a few steps.

Once you know the size of the position you are controlling, deciding how much to risk becomes a straightforward division. Most traders who blow up an account do not do it because they picked the wrong pair. They do it because they sized the position as if a lot were something small. It is not. A lot is a big bundle of currency, and the size of that bundle is what your entire risk calculation is built on.
