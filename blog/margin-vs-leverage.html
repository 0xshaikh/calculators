Margin vs Leverage: What's the Difference?

LotCal Blog5 min read

The two words everyone mixes up

Leverage and margin get used as if they were synonyms, and they are not. They are two sides of the same coin, and confusing them is one of the most common reasons new traders size positions far too large. So let us put the distinction down cleanly before it costs anyone money.

Leverage is a ratio. It tells you how much buying power you control compared to the money you actually put up. Margin is a real amount of money. It is the deposit your broker holds aside so the trade can exist. One is a multiplier, and the other is a number on your account statement.

Leverage is the multiplier

When a broker advertises 100:1 leverage, it means you can control 100 dollars of position for every 1 dollar of your own money. At 30:1, you control 30 dollars per dollar. At 10:1, you control 10. The leverage number does not tell you how much you will lose — it only tells you how much position you are allowed to hold for a given deposit.

Higher leverage is not a measure of skill or a reward. It is simply permission to control more currency. The market does not care what leverage you are on. A move of 50 pips is a move of 50 pips whether you are at 10:1 or 500:1. What changes is how much of your own money that move takes with it, because the position is bigger relative to your deposit.

Margin is the money set aside

Margin is the actual cash your broker locks up when you open a trade. If you open a position and your account shows a margin figure, that money is not floating or available to trade with — it is reserved as collateral for the position you are holding. When you close the trade, that margin is released back to your available balance.

There are a few related terms that all trace back to this. Required margin is the minimum your broker demands to open the trade. Free margin is what you have left over after the required margin is set aside, and it is what you can use to open more positions. Margin level is the ratio of your equity to your used margin, and it is the number brokers watch to decide when to start closing your trades for you.

The relationship in one formula

Leverage and margin are connected by a simple, exact relationship. The required margin is your position value divided by the leverage. Flip it around, and the effective leverage on an open trade is your position value divided by the margin you have set aside. If you have 1,000 dollars of position and 100 dollars of margin, you are at 10:1. If the margin were 10 dollars, you would be at 100:1.

That means the required margin percentage is the reciprocal of the leverage. At 100:1, the margin requirement is 1 percent. At 50:1, it is 2 percent. At 30:1, it is about 3.33 percent. If you ever see a broker say the margin requirement is 2 percent, that is the same thing as saying 50:1 leverage. They are two ways of writing the same number.

A worked example

Say you want to buy one lot of EURUSD at 1.0850, and your broker offers 30:1 leverage. The position value is 100,000 euros, which at 1.0850 is about $108,500. Divide that by 30, and the required margin is roughly $3,617. That is the money that gets set aside when you open the trade.

Now imagine the same trade on a 100:1 account. The position value is identical — still $108,500. But divide by 100, and the required margin drops to about $1,085. Same trade, same exposure, but your broker only asks for about a third of the deposit. This is the part that feels like a gift and is really just a bigger risk. The leverage did not make the trade safer; it made it cheaper to open, which means more of your balance is exposed to the same dollar swings.

Why the confusion causes over-sizing

Here is the trap. A trader hears that 100:1 leverage is "free buying power" and that margin is "just a small deposit," and concludes that the trade is low risk. But the position controls the same $108,500 either way. A 100-pip move against you is worth about $1,000 on that position, whether your margin was $3,617 or $1,085. The only thing the leverage changed is how much cushion you started with relative to that swing.

When people blow up accounts, it is rarely because they misunderstood a chart. It is usually because they treated a low margin requirement as a sign that the position was small. It was not. The position was the same size; they just got to hold it with less money backing it.

The clean rule of thumb

Keep the two ideas separate and the whole thing gets easier. Leverage is the multiplier the broker allows. Margin is the amount they actually take from you. And the number that should drive your position size is neither of them — it is the amount you are prepared to lose if the trade goes against you.

Pick your risk first, then work out the position that fits it, and let the margin figure be what it is. If you do that, it will not matter whether the account is 10:1 or 100:1. You will already know what the trade is worth to you in dollars if it fails, because that number was set before you ever clicked the button.
