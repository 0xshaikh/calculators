Bitcoin Futures Contract Size: 1 BTC, 0.001 BTC, and Everything Between

LotCal Blog5 min read

One lot is one coin

Of all the contract sizes you will ever look at, crypto is the simplest. For BTCUSD — bitcoin against the dollar — 1 lot = 1 bitcoin. There is no 100-ounce rule like gold and no 5,000-ounce rule like silver. One lot, one coin.

That simplicity is deceptive in a good way: it means the math is almost trivial and you can do most of it in your head. But there are a couple of wrinkles worth knowing — mostly about fractional lots, and about the fact that not every exchange uses the same convention.

Position value

Position value is the number of bitcoins you control, multiplied by the price. Since 1 lot is 1 BTC, a full lot at $100,000 is a $100,000 position. But you do not have to trade a whole lot:

  • 1.00 lot = 1 BTC → $100,000 at $100k.
  • 0.10 lot = 0.1 BTC → $10,000.
  • 0.01 lot = 0.01 BTC → $1,000.
  • 0.001 lot = 0.001 BTC → $100.

The "0.001 lot = 1 millibit" shortcut is worth remembering. A millibit is a thousandth of a coin, and it is the kind of fraction that lets a small account get involved in bitcoin without controlling a whole coin's worth of exposure.

Profit and loss per $1 move

Because 1 lot is exactly 1 BTC, a $1 move in the bitcoin price moves your P&L by exactly $1 per lot. The formula is the simplest you will ever use:

P&L per $1 move = lot size × 1

  • 1.00 lot → $1 per $1 of price movement.
  • 0.10 lot → $0.10 per $1.
  • 0.01 lot → $0.01 per $1.
  • 0.001 lot → $0.001 per $1.

Compare that to gold, where a full lot moves $100 per $1, or silver, where it moves $5,000. Crypto's per-dollar sensitivity is small precisely because one lot is one coin and a coin is worth a lot. The flip side is that bitcoin moves in big dollar chunks — a $2,000 day is ordinary — so the small per-dollar figure still adds up quickly.

A worked example

Say you have a $500 account and you buy 0.01 lot of bitcoin at $100,000. Position value: 0.01 × 100,000 = $1,000. You are controlling $1,000 with $500 — about 2× leverage.

Now the P&L. Bitcoin moves $1,000 in your favour. On 0.01 lot, that is 0.01 × 1000 = $10. A $10,000 move? That is $100, or 20% of your account. And if it moves $10,000 against you, you have lost $100 — a fifth of the account in a single swing that, for bitcoin, is not even unusual.

Here is the honest takeaway: the contract size is small, but bitcoin's daily range is not. On the same day a stock might move 1%, bitcoin can move 5% or more. That 5% on a $100,000 coin is $5,000 per coin — and on 0.01 lot it is $50 against a $500 account. It is the dollar moves, not the contract size, that make crypto feel wild.

Fractional lots let you scale in

Because lots are fractional and the contract is just one coin, you can dial your exposure almost continuously. Want to control $2,500 of bitcoin? That is 0.025 lot at $100k. Want $300? That is 0.003 lot. There is no minimum chunk the way there is with exchange-traded futures that force you to buy whole contracts.

This is the big advantage of the "1 lot = 1 BTC" convention: it is a clean unit that scales with your account, so you can risk a fixed percentage of your balance no matter how small that balance is.

Not all exchanges use 1:1

Here is the wrinkle. Not every venue defines a "contract" the same way. Some exchanges offer inverse contracts, where the profit is paid in bitcoin rather than dollars. Some use a contract multiplier so that a single contract represents a fraction of a coin — for example, one contract might be 0.001 BTC. Some quote in different units entirely, and some futures products are physically settled while others are cash settled.

So while "1 lot = 1 BTC" is the cleanest mental model, always check what your exchange's contract actually represents before you size a position. Leverage and margin: the other half of the story

The contract size tells you what you control, but it does not tell you what you need to put up. That part is the broker's margin requirement. If your exchange asks for 5% margin, then controlling a $100,000 position needs $5,000 of your own money. On 0.01 lot, where the position is $1,000, the same 5% would need just $50.

Here is the thing that trips people up: margin and contract size are separate, but they combine. A tiny lot with high leverage can still produce a liquidation on a normal day, because the leverage — not the lot number — determines how much adverse move you can absorb. On 0.01 lot at $100k, a $10,000 adverse move is $100 of loss. If your account is $500, that is 20%. If you are only putting up $50 of margin, the position would be liquidated well before the full move plays out.

That is why the useful questions are always the same: how much am I controlling, how much of my own money is behind it, and how far can the price move against me before the broker steps in.

Bottom line

Bitcoin contract size is the friendliest in trading: one lot, one coin. Position value is just the number of coins times the price, and P&L moves $1 per $1 per full lot, scaling down linearly from there. The only real traps are fractional lots (learn the millibit) and the fact that some exchanges use a different multiplier. Get those two right, and sizing a bitcoin position is genuinely easy.
