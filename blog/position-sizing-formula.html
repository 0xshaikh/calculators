Position Sizing Formulas: Fixed Fractional and Fixed Risk

LotCal Blog6 min read

Two ways to decide how much to risk

Every position sizing method starts with the same question: how much money are you willing to lose if the trade goes wrong? Once you answer that, the rest is arithmetic. There are two common ways to answer it, and they differ in one important way — what stays fixed when your account changes.

The first is fixed fractional. You risk a fixed percentage of your current balance on every trade — say 1 percent. The second is fixed risk. You risk the same dollar amount on every trade regardless of your balance — say $100.

Both are legitimate. Both keep you from betting more than you planned. But they behave differently as your account grows and shrinks, and knowing the difference tells you which one fits your goals.

Fixed fractional: risk a percentage of the balance

With fixed fractional sizing, the risk is a percentage of whatever your account is worth right now. A $10,000 account risking 1 percent puts $100 on the line. Win some trades and the account climbs to $12,000, so 1 percent is now $120. Lose a few and it drops to $8,000, so 1 percent is $80.

The percentage never changes, but the dollar figure breathes with the balance. That is the point. When the account is doing well, you risk a little more in absolute terms. When it is not, you automatically shrink. Nothing about your risk needs manual adjustment — it scales itself.

This is why fixed fractional is the default recommendation for most traders. It is conservative when it needs to be and generous when the account can afford it, and it never lets a single trade take more than the fixed slice.

Fixed risk: risk the same dollar amount

Fixed risk is simpler to think about. You decide that every trade risks $100, or $500, or whatever number you choose, and that is that. The stop and the position size get worked out around that fixed number. Your balance does not enter the calculation at all.

The advantage is predictability. You always know exactly what a loss will cost you. This makes it easy to track and easy to reason about: ten losing trades in a row cost ten times your fixed risk, no more and no less.

The disadvantage is that it does not scale. If your account doubles, you are still risking the same dollar amount — which means you are now risking a smaller percentage. If your account halves, you are risking the same dollars — which is now a larger percentage of a smaller account, and could suddenly be too much. Fixed risk treats a $10,000 account and a $5,000 account identically, and that can be a problem.

The core formula

Both methods converge on the same formula once you know the dollar risk. Position size comes from dividing the risk by how much the trade would lose over the distance of your stop.

Position size = risk amount ÷ (stop distance in price × contract size)

The denominator — stop distance times contract size — is simply the dollar loss per contract if the stop is hit. Dividing your risk by that number tells you how many contracts to trade.

The key is keeping the units straight. Stop distance must be in price units (dollars per ounce for gold, pips for forex). Contract size must be in the same units that turn price movement into dollars (ounces per contract for gold, units per lot for forex). Multiply them and you get dollars lost per contract at your stop.

Worked example: gold

You have a $10,000 account and you want to risk 1 percent, which is $100. Gold is trading near $2,000 per ounce, and you want your stop 5 dollars per ounce away — that is 5 points on the chart.

A full COMEX gold contract is 100 ounces. If price moves $5 against you, the loss per contract is 5 × 100 = $500. That is your denominator.

Position size = $100 ÷ $500 = 0.2 contracts. On a platform offering micro gold — 10 ounces per contract — the same trade is 2 micro contracts, because 2 × 10 × 5 = $100.

Notice how the contract size matters. A 5-dollar stop on a full gold contract risks $500 per contract, so a $100 budget only buys a fifth of a contract. Ignore the contract size and you would think the trade is twice as big as it really is.

Worked example: forex

Now forex. You have a $5,000 account and you want to risk 1 percent, which is $50. On EURUSD your stop is 20 pips from your entry.

One standard lot of EURUSD is 100,000 units, and one pip on a standard lot is worth about $10. So a 20-pip stop on one standard lot risks 20 × 10 = $200.

Position size = $50 ÷ $200 = 0.25 lots. That is the answer: a $5,000 account, a 1 percent risk, a 20-pip stop, and the trade is a quarter of a lot.

This is why position sizing never feels like guessing — because it is not. You started from the money you were willing to lose and let the stop and the contract size produce the lot count.

Pros and cons

Fixed fractional — risk a fixed percentage of current balance:

  • Pros: scales automatically with your account; never risks more than the fixed slice; the standard choice for compounding growth.
  • Cons: the dollar risk changes every trade, so you have to recalculate each time; after a drawdown the dollar risk is smaller, which can feel slow on the way back up.

Fixed risk — risk a fixed dollar amount:

  • Pros: trivially predictable; the same loss every time; easy to plan around.
  • Cons: does not scale — a growing account risks a shrinking percentage and a shrinking account risks a growing one; can overbet a shrunken account without you noticing.

Neither is right or wrong. Fixed fractional is the better fit for most retail traders because it ties risk to the account that is actually carrying it. Fixed risk is useful if you have a specific dollar figure you are comfortable losing, or if you are testing a strategy and want identical conditions across every trade.
