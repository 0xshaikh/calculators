Position Sizing: How Much Should You Risk Per Trade?

LotCal Blog6 min read

The one number that decides everything

Ask a room of traders what matters most and you will hear about entries, exits, indicators, and strategy. But there is one number that quietly decides whether you survive a losing streak: how much of your account you risk on a single trade. Everything else is detail. This is the number.

Position sizing is the process of turning that risk decision into an actual order size. It is not about guessing how big a trade feels right. It is arithmetic. You decide how much you are willing to lose, you measure how far away your stop is, and you divide the first by the second. That division is the whole job.

Risk as a percentage of your balance

The most widely used rule is to risk a small, fixed percentage of your account on each trade — usually between 1 and 2 percent. On a $5,000 account, 1 percent is $50. On a $10,000 account, it is $100. The percentage stays constant, but the dollar amount grows with your balance, which is exactly what you want. As the account grows, you risk more in dollars but the same relative amount, so a losing trade never deals a disproportionate blow.

The reason for a small fixed percentage is not timidity. It is that losing streaks happen to everyone, even good traders. If you risk 10 percent per trade, four losses in a row take a huge chunk out of the account. If you risk 1 percent, four losses in a row cost you about four percent, which is annoying but survivable. The difference between the two is not the strategy — it is whether the account is still standing to take the next trade.

The core formula

Position size comes from one clean relationship: risk equals stop distance times position size. If you know any two of the three, you can solve for the third, and the one you usually want is position size. Rearrange it and you get position size equals risk amount divided by stop distance.

Stop distance needs a definition before it is useful. In forex, you measure it in pips. A stop that is 20 pips from your entry is a stop distance of 20 pips. In stocks or futures, you might measure it in points or dollars of price movement. Whatever the unit, the point is that it has to be measured in the same unit as the risk — in pips if you are sizing a forex position, in dollars per share if you are sizing an equity position.

A worked example, step by step

Let us take a concrete case. You have a $1,000 account and you want to risk 1 percent per trade, which is $10. You see a setup on EURUSD where your stop needs to sit 20 pips from your entry. Now the arithmetic: divide the $10 risk by the 20-pip stop, and you get 50 cents of risk per pip. You are allowed to risk 50 cents on each pip the trade moves against you.

The next step is converting that into a lot size. On one standard lot of EURUSD, a pip is worth about $10. You are allowed to risk 50 cents per pip, which is one twentieth of that. So the position is 0.05 lots. That is the answer: a $1,000 account, a 1 percent risk, a 20-pip stop, and the trade is 0.05 lots.

Notice what happened. We never guessed. We never looked at a chart and decided the position felt right. We started from the money we were willing to lose, measured the stop, and let the division produce the size. The trade is as big as it is because the risk budget says so — not because the setup looked tempting.

Why size matters more than win rate

A surprising amount of trading talk centers on win rate, and it is the wrong thing to obsess over. A trader with a 40 percent win rate can be very profitable if the winners are three times the size of the losers. A trader with a 70 percent win rate can go broke if the losers are three times the size of the winners. Win rate alone tells you nothing; the ratio of average win to average loss is what matters.

Position sizing is what makes that ratio work in your favor. When you risk a fixed small percentage, a losing trade costs you a fixed small percentage. It cannot spiral. A winning trade might earn more than that, and over time the good trades outweigh the bad ones. But if position sizing is wrong, even a strong edge gets buried under a single oversized loss.

The formula in one line

If you remember nothing else, remember this: risk amount divided by stop distance equals position size. Decide the risk as a percentage of balance, measure the stop, and divide. That is the entire method, and it takes about thirty seconds with a calculator.

There is one more thing worth saying. The 1 to 2 percent range is a guideline, not a law. The real requirement is that you pick a number and stick to it consistently. If you risk 2 percent every single time, that is disciplined. If you risk 0.5 percent one day and 15 percent the next because a trade felt important, that is not position sizing — that is gambling. The number matters less than the consistency, and consistency is what keeps you in the game long enough to get good.
