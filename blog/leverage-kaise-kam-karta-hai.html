How Leverage Actually Works: Margin, Liquidation, and What the Broker Lends

LotCal Blog7 min read

Margin: The Money You Actually Put Up

When you open a leveraged trade, you do not put up the full value of the position. You put up a slice of it, and that slice is called margin. Margin is not a fee and it is not a cost. It is your own money, held aside as a deposit, and it comes back to you — along with any profit or minus any loss — when you close the trade.

The margin requirement is usually expressed as a percentage. If the requirement is 1%, you need to put up 1% of the position's value. If it is 5%, you put up 5%. The rest is effectively covered by the broker.

What the Broker Actually Lends You

Here is the honest way to think about leverage. The broker lends you the difference between the position's value and your margin. If you want to control $10,000 and you put up $100, the broker covers the other $9,900. Your leverage is 100:1, usually written as 1:100.

You never see this loan the way you would see a bank loan. There is no monthly payment and no interest rate shown to you. The loan is implicit in the position. But it is real, and it is the reason your profit and loss are calculated on the full position size rather than just on your margin.

This is why the ratio matters so much. At 1:100, a 1% move in the price is a 100% move on your margin. The broker's loan magnifies both directions.

Initial Margin and Maintenance Margin

Brokers and exchanges usually draw a line between two levels of margin.

Initial margin is what you need in your account to open the position. This is the deposit the broker requires before the trade is allowed to exist.

Maintenance margin is the lower amount your account must keep afterwards. Because your balance changes with the price of the position, your margin level is constantly moving. If your balance falls but stays above the maintenance requirement, the trade continues. If it falls below, the broker steps in.

The gap between initial and maintenance margin is your cushion. It is the amount of room the broker gives you before it acts.

What Happens on a Margin Call

A margin call is the broker telling you that your account has dropped below the required level. In practice, with most modern retail brokers, this is not a phone call. It is a warning on your screen, and sometimes the broker simply acts without asking.

When a margin call happens, you usually have two choices: add more money to your account, or reduce or close the position. If you do neither, the broker will close positions for you to protect its loan.

What Liquidation Means

Liquidation is the broker closing your position automatically because the loss has reached the point where your margin can no longer cover it. It is the point at which the broker's loan is at risk, so the broker takes back its money by closing the trade and collecting what remains of your margin.

It is worth being clear about what liquidation is not. It is not a fee, it is not a penalty, and it is not the broker being unfair. It is the mechanism that keeps a leveraged system working. Without it, a trader could lose far more than they ever deposited, which is not something any broker will allow.

A Worked Liquidation Example

Let us make this concrete with numbers. Suppose you have $1,000 in your account and you open a position at 1:100 leverage. That position is worth $100,000.

Position value = $1,000 × 100 = $100,000

Now the price moves against you by 1%. The loss on the full position is:

Loss = $100,000 × 1% = $1,000

That loss is exactly equal to your entire margin. Your account is down to zero, and the broker liquidates the position. You lose your $1,000.

Now the same trade at 1:20 leverage. With $1,000, your position is worth $20,000. A 1% adverse move costs $200, and you still have $800 left. To wipe out the account, the price would need to move against you by 5%:

Adverse move that wipes margin = 1 ÷ leverage

1 ÷ 20 = 5%

This is the single most useful number to understand about leverage. The higher the ratio, the smaller the adverse move that ends the trade.

Why the Broker Sets These Rules

All of this is not there to trip you up. The broker is lending real money, and it needs a way to guarantee it gets back. The margin requirement is the collateral, and liquidation is the enforcement. The rules are the same kind of rules any lender would apply: if the collateral loses value, the lender acts.

From your side, the practical lesson is to know your margin level at all times, and to understand that the price move that will end your trade is simply 1 divided by your leverage.
