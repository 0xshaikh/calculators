7 Lot Size Mistakes Beginners Make (and How to Avoid Them)

LotCal Blog7 min read

Most losing trades are not bad trades. They are good trades sized badly. A setup that would have been a modest win becomes a painful loss simply because the lot size was wrong. The frustrating part is that every one of these mistakes is avoidable — they all come down to a few bits of arithmetic that are easy to get right once you know what to check.

Here are the seven lot size mistakes I see most often, and the fix for each.

1. Using the wrong contract size

Every instrument has its own contract size, and they are not interchangeable. Silver does not trade like gold, and forex does not trade like crypto. If you assume silver uses 100 ounces per lot when it actually uses 5,000, your position value will be off by a factor of fifty — which means your risk is fifty times what you intended.

The fix: read the contract size from your broker's instrument specifications before you size anything. Treat it as non-negotiable. If you are not sure, look it up or use a lot size calculator that asks for the contract size explicitly rather than assuming one.

2. A lot that is too big for your balance

This is the classic beginner mistake, and it is almost always emotional. The trade looks good, so you size up to make the win feel bigger. But the lot size that feels exciting is usually the lot size that wipes out your account in one bad streak. A single large loss on an over-sized position can erase weeks of careful work.

The fix: size from risk, not from excitement. Decide how much of your balance you are willing to lose on one trade — a common rule of thumb is 1% — and let that number set your lot size. The lot should be the output, never the input.

3. Ignoring pip and point value

Your lot size tells you the position value, but your profit or loss on a stop-out is measured in pips and points. A 1.00 lot gold trade and a 1.00 lot EURUSD trade have very different pip values, even if their position values look similar. If you size two trades to the same lot but ignore how much each pip is worth, your risk is different on each one — and you will not know it until the trade is closed.

The fix: convert your stop distance into money before you place the order. Take the pip or point value of the instrument at your chosen lot size and multiply it by the number of pips in your stop. That tells you the actual dollar risk of the trade. Only then can you compare it against your risk budget.

4. Using your balance as your position value

It is easy to glance at a $10,000 balance and think a trade with a few thousand dollars of exposure is small. But position value and balance are different things. A $10,000 balance can comfortably support a $2,400 position value — yet the same balance is nowhere near enough for a $240,000 position value, even though both are "small" relative to some other accounts. Confusing the two is how a modest-looking lot turns into a margin call.

The fix: always think in position value first, then check the margin required against your balance. Position value is what you are exposed to; margin is what your broker locks up; balance is what you actually have. Do not let them blur together.

5. Adding lots mid-trade without re-calculating

Scaling into a position feels smart: buy a little, and if it goes your way, add more. The problem is that most traders add lots without re-checking their total exposure. Your average entry moves, your position value grows, and your risk — which was fine at 0.10 lots — is suddenly much larger at 0.30 lots. The extra size is never free.

The fix: treat every add as a brand new trade. Re-calculate the total position value, the new average price, and the risk to your stop before adding. If the combined position would exceed your risk budget, do not add. Size the whole plan, not just the first entry.

6. Ignoring the margin and leverage cap

Leverage does not give you free buying power — it gives you borrowed buying power. A 1:500 leverage account makes it very easy to place a lot size that your margin cannot actually support. When price moves against you even a little, the margin call arrives fast, and you are out of the trade at the worst moment.

The fix: check the margin required for your position value at your broker's leverage before you submit. If you need $800 in margin and you have $600, the trade does not fit. A quick margin check takes ten seconds and prevents the most expensive surprise in trading.

7. Forgetting fees and spread on small lots

Small lots have a sneaky cost: the spread and commission do not shrink in proportion. On a 0.01 lot, the fixed costs of a trade can eat a noticeable slice of a small profit. If your stop is tight and your lot is tiny, you can find that the trade was never going to be profitable because the costs take more than the expected move.

The fix: include costs in your sizing. Estimate the spread and commission as a dollar amount for your lot size, and make sure your expected profit comfortably exceeds them. If the costs are more than a meaningful fraction of your target, the trade is not worth taking at that size — either widen the target or skip it.

Wrapping up

All seven mistakes share a root cause: the lot size was chosen before the numbers were checked. Reverse the order — check the contract size, the pip value, the margin, and the costs — and most of these problems disappear on their own. You do not need to be a mathematician to do it, just a trader who is willing to spend thirty seconds calculating before clicking the button.
