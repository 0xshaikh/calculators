Lot Size Explained: Standard, Mini, and Micro Lots

LotCal Blog6 min read

If you have opened a trading platform and stared at the lot size box, you already know the feeling: a small dropdown asking you to pick 1.00, 0.10, or 0.01, with no real explanation of what those numbers mean. That little box quietly controls how much money you are putting behind every trade. Getting it right matters more than almost anything else on the screen.

This article breaks down the three standard lot sizes, what each one controls, how to convert between them, and why brokers let you trade tiny fractions in the first place.

What a lot actually is

A lot is simply a fixed unit of trading quantity. Instead of telling your broker "I want to buy 50,000 euros," you say "I want to buy 0.50 lots," and the broker knows exactly what that means because the lot size is defined for you.

The idea exists because markets deal in huge amounts of money. A single currency transaction between two banks can be millions of units, so the industry created a shorthand to avoid typing long numbers all day. Over time, that shorthand spread to every instrument, and now lots are the universal way traders size their positions.

The three standard sizes

Standard lot (1.00)

One standard lot is the biggest common size, and it is the one most pricing is built around.

  • Forex: 1 lot = 100,000 units of the base currency. So 1.00 lot of EURUSD means you are trading 100,000 euros.
  • Gold: 1 lot = 100 troy ounces. A 1.00 lot gold position controls 100 ounces of the metal.
  • Crypto: for the major pairs like BTCUSD, 1 lot = 1 full coin. One lot of BTCUSD controls 1 bitcoin.

A standard lot is a large position. On a $10,000 account, a single 1.00 lot forex trade is roughly ten times your balance in notional value — which is why most retail traders never touch it unless they have a big account or very high leverage.

Mini lot (0.10)

A mini lot is one tenth of a standard lot.

  • Forex: 0.10 lot = 10,000 units.
  • Gold: 0.10 lot = 10 ounces.
  • Crypto: 0.10 lot = 0.10 of a coin.

Mini lots exist for people who want smaller, more manageable risk. If a standard lot moves too much for your account, a mini lot gives you the same trade with one tenth of the exposure. It is the most common starting point for traders building up from a small balance.

Micro lot (0.01)

A micro lot is one hundredth of a standard lot.

  • Forex: 0.01 lot = 1,000 units.
  • Gold: 0.01 lot = 1 ounce.
  • Crypto: 0.01 lot = 0.01 of a coin.

Micro lots are the training wheels of trading. With a 0.01 lot, a single pip move on a major pair is worth roughly a tenth of a dollar, which means you can practice sizing and risk without watching your account swing wildly. Many brokers now go even further, offering 0.001 or nano lots for the smallest possible positions.

What each lot controls

Here is the key thing to internalise: the lot size does not control price. It controls position value. Price moves the same whether you trade 0.01 or 1.00 lots. What changes is how much of that move you are exposed to.

Think of it like a magnifying glass. The price move is the object under the glass. The lot size is the magnification. A bigger lot magnifies the same price movement into a bigger change in your account, in both directions. That is why a 1.00 lot gold trade and a 0.01 lot gold trade both show the same percentage move on the chart — but the larger lot moves your balance far more.

How to convert between lots

Conversion is simple because the sizes are clean multiples of ten and one hundred.

  • Standard to mini: multiply by 10. So 1.00 lot = 10 mini lots.
  • Mini to micro: multiply by 10. So 1 mini lot = 10 micro lots.
  • Standard to micro: multiply by 100. So 1.00 lot = 100 micro lots.
  • Going the other way, just divide: 5 mini lots = 0.50 lots, and 25 micro lots = 0.25 lots.

The pattern is always the same. Drop or add a zero. If you can move a decimal point, you can convert lots.

Why brokers let you trade fractions

You might wonder why anyone would trade 0.37 lots instead of a clean 0.50. The answer is precision. If you risk a fixed percentage of your account per trade — say 1% — the exact lot size that fits that risk is rarely a round number. A trader with $1,400 risking 1% on a trade with a 20-pip stop might need 0.07 lots. Fractional lots let you size to the trade instead of the other way around, so your risk stays consistent even as your balance changes.

A worked example

Suppose you want to trade gold at $2,400 per ounce with a 0.10 lot. Your position value is:

0.10 lot × 100 oz × $2,400 = $24,000

So a 0.10 lot gold trade controls $24,000 worth of gold. If you instead used 0.01 lot, the same trade would control $2,400. You can see how quickly the exposure scales — moving from micro to mini to standard multiplies your position value by ten each time. That is the whole point: lots are just a compact way to express how much value you are putting to work.

Wrapping up

Lot sizes are the vocabulary of position sizing. Once you know that 1.00 means standard, 0.10 means mini, and 0.01 means micro — and that each one controls a specific amount of value — the dropdown stops being a mystery.
