Five Simple Risk Management Rules That Actually Work

LotCal Blog6 min read

Every futures trader has heard the same advice: cut your losses, let your winners run. Easy to say, hard to do when a position is moving against you and your screen is flashing red. The difference between traders who survive their first year and those who don't usually comes down to a handful of small, boring decisions made before the trade, not clever analysis made during it.

Here are five rules you can write down on an index card and actually follow. Each one is simple enough to apply on your very next trade, and each exists for a concrete reason.

1. Risk a fixed fraction of your balance

The single most important decision in any trade is not which direction to take — it is how much you are willing to lose if you are wrong. Fixed fractional risk means you risk the same small percentage of your account on every trade, usually 1% or 2%.

Say your account has $10,000. At 1% risk, your maximum loss on any single trade is $100. At 2%, it is $200. When the account grows to $12,000, those numbers rise to $120 and $240. When it drops to $8,000, they fall to $80 and $160. The risk scales with your balance, so a losing streak never grows faster than the account that has to absorb it.

Why it works: it keeps any single mistake from doing serious damage. A 2% risk per trade means you would need 25 losses in a row before you hit 50% of your account — and 25 losses in a row is almost always a sign that your strategy itself is broken, not just bad luck. Fixed fractional risk also protects you from the trap of "risking more to get back what I lost," which is how small losses become account-ending ones.

2. Place your stop-loss before you enter

A stop-loss is not an optional safety net you add later. It is part of the trade itself. Decide where you are wrong before you click buy or sell, and enter that stop with the order. If the market hits your level, you are out — no debate, no hesitation, no "just give it one more tick."

The stop belongs somewhere the market has to prove you wrong, not a random round number. If you are buying at a support level, your stop might go a few ticks below that support. If you are shorting a resistance level, the stop goes above it. The exact placement matters less than the fact that it exists before the trade is on.

Why it works: it removes the worst decision you will ever make — deciding whether to exit while the trade is already losing. Emotional decisions made mid-trade are almost always late and almost always too generous. A pre-placed stop turns a scary situation into a routine one: the market moves, the stop fills, the loss is small and already accounted for in your fixed risk.

3. Stop for the day after about 3R

One losing trade is fine. Two is uncomfortable. Three or more in a single session is a signal that something is off — the market may be in a choppy regime your strategy does not handle, or you may be forcing trades out of frustration. Either way, the right move is to close the laptop and come back tomorrow.

Using "R" makes this concrete. R is your fixed risk per trade. If you risk 1% per trade, a 3R daily loss means you are down 3% for the day. Hit that number and you stop. No exceptions, no "one more trade to win it back."

Why it works: it caps your worst day. A 3R daily limit means your biggest possible single-day drawdown is fixed in advance, so no one session can undo a week of good work. It also protects you from tilt — the state of mind where losses make you take bigger, faster, sloppier trades to recover. Tilt is the fastest way to blow up an account, and a hard daily stop is the simplest guard against it.

4. Take a pause after two consecutive losses

Two losses in a row is not the end of the world. But it is the moment to slow down. Step away from the screen for at least 30 minutes, review what happened, and check whether your setups were actually valid or whether you were chasing trades that did not meet your criteria.

This rule is stricter than the daily stop and applies even on a good day. You can lose two trades early in the session and still be up overall — but the pause still matters, because the second loss often comes from impatience after the first. Break the pattern before it forms.

Why it works: it interrupts the emotional spiral. Losses are rarely random events. The first loss creates a small urge to recover, which makes you take a marginally worse trade, which makes the second loss bigger and the urge stronger. A forced pause breaks that loop while it is still cheap. Many traders find that after the pause, the market looks completely different — and the trade they were about to force looks obviously wrong.

5. Never add to a losing position

Averaging down — buying more as price falls — feels like it makes your entry better. In practice it makes your position bigger while the market is telling you your first idea was wrong. If your stop is at 1R and you add another contract at a worse price, your total loss is no longer 1R. It is 2R, 3R, or more, and your stop is now far away enough to hurt.

The rule is absolute: if a trade is losing, you do not add. You either take the small loss at your stop, or you do nothing. Adding to a loser turns a defined-risk trade into an undefined-risk trade, and undefined risk is exactly what futures can punish.

Why it works: it keeps every trade within your fixed risk budget. The whole point of rules 1 and 2 is that you know your worst case before you enter. Adding to a loser quietly throws that away. By refusing to add, you keep the account's exposure predictable and you stay mentally honest about the trade you actually made, not the one you wish you had made.

Putting it together

None of these rules will tell you which direction to trade, and none of them guarantee profits. What they do is make your losses small, predictable, and survivable — which is what keeps you in the game long enough for your good trades to matter. Set your fixed risk, place your stop first, cap your daily loss, pause after two losses, and never add to a loser. Write them down, and check them off on every single trade.
